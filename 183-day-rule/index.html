The 183-day rule

There is no single 183-day rule. Many countries and US states use a figure near 183 days, but each counts days its own way, over its own period, and days are rarely the whole test. Here is how the main ones work, with the official source for each.

Not the same as Schengen 90/180. That is an immigration rule about how long you may stay; the 183-day rules are about tax and residence. For Schengen, use the Schengen calculator.

Two questions decide the count

What counts as a day?

Most rules count any part of a day: arrive at 23:00 and that day counts. The UK counts only days on which you are in the country at midnight. Portugal counts nights.

“183 or more”, or “more than 183”?

The US federal test and Canada need 183 days or more. New York and Spain need more than 183, so 184. One day makes the difference, so the wording matters.

Days are only part of the test

Most countries also look at your permanent home, your family, your work and where your life is centred. You can be resident with fewer days, or not resident with more. When two countries both treat you as resident, the tax treaty between them decides, using its own tie-breaker tests.

Tax treaties also have their own 183-day rule for employees, which decides where salary is taxed. It is a different test from the residence rules above.

Keep a record that holds up

In a residency audit, you usually have to prove your days. New York’s auditors ask for phone records, card statements and calendars, and you must show fewer than 184 days. A day-by-day record made at the time, with receipts or boarding passes attached, is far easier to defend than a list rebuilt later from memory.
